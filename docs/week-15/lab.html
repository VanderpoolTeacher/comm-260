Part 1 · Load your page on a device that is not yours

Before anything else in this session.

Open your published page on a machine you did not build it on, on a network that is not yours.

"It works on my machine" is not published.

A page that will not load is the most avoidable way to lose 8% of a course grade, and there is no week 16. If it fails here, you have the session to fix it. If it fails during your presentation, you do not.

Part 2 · Run the removal test, on your own page

Three deletions. Three findings. Written down.

  1. Remove the photography. What broke?
  2. Remove the audio. What broke?
  3. Remove the video. What broke?

Actually delete. Actually look at what is left. Not in your head — this is exactly what week 11 asked, and answering from intuition was not acceptable then either.

A deletion that breaks nothing is the finding, not the failure.

A page that fails with a correct diagnosis is worth more than a page that claims to pass and was never tested. That is in the competency rubric and it has been true since week 11.

The two failures are unchanged:

Two media saying the same thing A positioning statement that describes the video. Audio narrating what the photographs show
The decorative third A still used as a header image, present because a page needs a header

Part 3 · Identify your structure, then price it

One question:

Does anything on your page assume something else was seen first?

| Yes | You built something linear and put it on a web page | | No | You built something non-linear |

Neither is wrong. Not noticing is.

Now name the cost. You will have the gain ready — everyone does, and week 10 established that the other half has to be asked for every single time.

Not a cost A cost
"You lose control, which lets the visitor choose." "Someone can land on my video and never see the statement explaining what any of it is for."
"It's linear, so it's more focused." "If they stop after the photographs, the audio and video did not happen."

If it sounds like a feature, it is not a cost.

Part 4 · Read your positioning statement back as a stranger

300–500 words. Who the work is for, and what conventions you follow.

Two checks:

  1. Is it a positioning statement, or a reflection? Nothing about what you learned, how you felt, or what you would do differently belongs in it. It is written for someone deciding whether to hire you.
  2. Does your work actually follow the conventions you claim? Open the page beside the statement. A statement claiming conventions your media does not follow is worse than one claiming fewer — because now the page contradicts its own account of itself.

Claim less and be right.

Part 5 · Critique — the fourth and final time

Technical quality. Aesthetic design. Narrative effectiveness. One specific, actionable point each.

The test is week 4's and it has not changed: does the person receiving it know what to do next?

Part 6 · Prepare a defence, not a walkthrough

Use the presentation prep sheet — the one you were given in week 12.

5–7 minutes, plus questions. You will be asked at least two questions you did not prepare for. That is not a hazard of the format; it is the assessment.

Expect to be asked:

  • Why a specific image is cropped the way it is
  • Why that structure, and what it cost
  • What breaks if a medium is removed — and it may be checked live
  • A departure from your week 14 plan
  • What you would change with another week

The answer that fails: "It looked better."

That is week 1's failure in the final week of the course. Have a reason for anything you would be surprised to be asked about — the surprises are where the assessment happens.

Two grades, and they never mix. The project rubric scores components A–H at 85%. The presentation rubric scores component I at 15%. A good presentation cannot rescue a page that does not work, and a working page does not excuse a defence you cannot give.
