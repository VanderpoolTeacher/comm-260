Week 3 · Shooting to intent — Lesson

COMM 260 · Introduction to Digital Media Production

Read this before the session. About 20 minutes. Bring a draft intent in writing.

Week 2 taught you to see composition in other people's work. This week you make it — and find out whether it survives contact with someone who was not in your head.

1 · What a creative intent is

One paragraph, written before you shoot, stating what the images are meant to do.

Not what you will photograph. What the pictures should accomplish.

This is a topic This is an intent
"I'm going to photograph the river" "I want these to show the river as a working place — people using it, not looking at it"

The left names a subject. The right tells you what to shoot, what to leave out, and how you will know if it worked.

The two ways a draft fails

Too broad to fail. "To show emotion." Any six images satisfy it. An intent that cannot be missed cannot be served either.

A topic, not an intent. "Portraits of my roommate." Says what is in front of the camera and nothing about what the pictures are for.

The test

What set of images would fail this intent?

If you cannot answer that, it is too broad. A usable intent excludes things.

The glossary bars concept, vision, artist intent, and mood as substitutes. They are vaguer words for the same job, and vagueness is the failure mode here.

2 · Composition becomes a means

Everything from week 2 — placement, negative space, leading the eye, cropping, depth of field — is now a means to an end you stated in advance.

The question stops being is this well composed. It becomes:

Does this composition serve what I said I was doing?

You will find this counter-intuitive. A technically strong image that contradicts your intent is a failure this week. Some of your best frames will not make the set.

3 · Emphasis you build, not emphasis you notice

Week 2 asked you to see subject emphasis and rank its means. This week you construct it.

Outcome 3.5: at least two of placement, scale, focus, contrast, and light — in the same frame.

Some combinations:

  • Subject placed off-centre and lit brighter than its surroundings
  • Subject small in the frame and the only thing sharp
  • Subject in shadow and the only warm thing in a cool picture

Why two and not one

One means can happen by accident. A subject ends up sharp because it was closest to the camera. You did not decide that; the lens did.

Two, chosen together, is a decision. That is what this outcome is actually measuring — deliberateness, not skill.

Be able to say which two you used, per frame.

4 · Selecting down is where the intent lands

You will shoot at least twenty-five frames and submit six.

Most of the intent-serving happens in that cut, not in the shooting. Two questions per frame:

  1. Does it serve the intent?
  2. Does it repeat something another frame already does better?

The hard part

A strong image that does not serve the intent weakens the set.

Cutting a good photograph feels wrong and it is the work. Six images should read as one body of work — not as six separate successes that happen to share a folder.

That is outcome 3.4, and it is the difference between a portfolio and a pile.

5 · The blind swap

You hand a partner six images only. You do not tell them your intent.

They write what they think the images were meant to do. Then you read your actual intent aloud.

The gap between those two is the assessment.

A small gap means the images carried it. A large gap means they did not — and that is information, not failure. You can often close it without reshooting at all, just by changing which six you chose.

Your partner cannot be told first. The explanation destroys the only evidence outcome 3.3 has.

Module 2 vocabulary — additions

Week 2's eleven terms remain in play. This week adds:

Term Means
Creative intent One paragraph, written before shooting, stating what the images are meant to do
Capture settings Aperture, shutter, ISO, white balance, recorded at the time of shooting
Tools statement What you used, what you set, and why those settings for that situation

The tools statement starts this week and never stops. Every project from here to week 15 carries one. It is the evidence for LO 2, and what counts is the reason, not the list:

"f/2.8 to drop the cluttered background behind her" — competency

"f/2.8, 1/250, ISO 400" — record-keeping

Check yourself before the session

  1. What is the difference between a topic and a creative intent?
  2. What is the test for an intent that is too broad?
  3. Why two means of emphasis rather than one?
  4. You shoot twenty-five frames and submit six. Where does most of the intent-serving happen?
  5. Your partner reads your six images and gets your intent completely wrong. Is that a failure?
  6. What makes a tools statement evidence rather than record-keeping?

If you cannot answer 5, re-read section 5.

Practise

Run the intent triage before the session — sort real draft intents into topic, too broad, and works, then repair the broken ones. Unlimited attempts, no marks.

What happens in the session

Intents are collected before anyone shoots. Then you shoot, select down, and swap blind. Full detail in the lab sheet and the assignment.

Keep your full shoot. Week 4 edits this material, and a later week will ask you for a photograph you made this term.
