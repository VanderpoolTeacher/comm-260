Week 11 · Integration — Lesson

COMM 260 · Introduction to Digital Media Production

Read this before the session. About 20 minutes.

Bring three things you have already made: a photograph from week 3, your audio piece from week 6, your video from week 9. On storage you control, and confirm they open before you arrive. This is the week's entire raw material and there is no substitute for it.

Also watch the provided example — two minutes, narration over pictures — and write one sentence on what you would lose by deleting the narration.

The answer is nothing. You should arrive having noticed that yourself.

1 · Ten weeks of separate media, and one question

You have made photographs, audio, and video. Each in its own module, each assessed on its own terms.

This week asks whether they can be one thing.

And the test for that is not a matter of opinion, which is unusual for this course:

The removal test

Delete one medium. If nothing breaks, the media were assembled rather than integrated.

That is it. It is performable, it gives an answer, and the answer is frequently not the one you wanted.

2 · Two states, and nothing between them

Means The test says
Integrated The media are interdependent. Removing one breaks the piece Delete any one → something is lost
Assembled The media are merely co-present Delete one → nothing changes

The glossary bars combined, mixed, multimedia, and blended for the first, and layered, stacked, and thrown together for the second. The whole outcome lives in that distinction, and each of those words quietly blurs it.

There is no partial credit in the concept itself — a medium either carries something or it does not. There is partial credit in the assessment, and it goes to diagnosis. More on that in section 6.

3 · Where interdependence actually comes from

You cannot make three media interdependent by wanting them to be. Interdependence follows from division of labour — and that means knowing what each one can do that the other two cannot.

Medium Can do what the others cannot
Photograph Holds a single moment still, long enough to be examined. You can look at it for as long as you like, and it does not move on
Audio Carries interiority — a voice says what someone thinks, which no image can show. And it works while the eye is busy elsewhere
Video Carries change over time, and therefore causation. This happened, and then because of it, that happened

Read that table as a set of jobs rather than a set of formats.

If each medium is doing the job only it can do, removing any one of them takes something with it. That is not a technique. It is a consequence.

4 · The failure you will produce by default

Two media saying the same thing.

Narration describing what is on screen. A photograph of the person whose voice we are hearing, doing the thing they are describing. A video of the place the audio just told us about.

Both present. Neither necessary. Delete either one and nothing is lost, because the other one already said it.

You will do this, and it will feel like good work while you are doing it, because redundancy feels like coherence. When the picture and the words agree, the piece feels tight and deliberate. It is not tight — it is doubled.

Two media saying the same thing Two media doing different jobs
Voice: "the workshop was freezing" over a shot of someone in a coat Voice: "I've been doing this thirty years" over a shot of hands that have
Photograph of the person speaking Photograph of what they refuse to talk about
Video of the thing the audio just described Video of what happened next

The right-hand column is harder to make and it is the only column that survives a deletion.

5 · The decorative third

There is a second failure, and photography is usually the casualty.

A still used as a background. A title card. An image sitting behind the video because the brief said three media and there were only two doing work.

It is present. It carries nothing. Delete it and the piece is unchanged — often slightly improved.

Watch for this in your own work specifically, because it is the failure that looks most like success: all three media are visibly there.

6 · Diagnosis is the harder half, and it is worth more

Outcome 11.3 is diagnose why a piece reads as assembled rather than integrated.

Detecting the failure is the removal test — you delete, and you observe. Anyone can do that.

Diagnosing it means saying which two media are duplicating each other, or which one was added to satisfy a requirement, or which job you thought a medium was doing that it turned out not to be doing.

Here is the thing to understand before you build anything:

A correctly diagnosed failure outranks an untested claim.

That is in the competency rubric, in those words.

A student whose piece does not survive, but who says precisely why — "my audio and my video are both about the same thirty seconds of that afternoon, and the audio is the weaker account of it" — has demonstrated the harder outcome.

A student who asserts their piece survives without performing the deletions has demonstrated nothing at all.

So do not protect the piece. Test it.

7 · And when a deletion breaks nothing

The instinct is to add. Told that your audio is removable, you will want to add more audio.

The fix is almost always subtraction.

If your audio is removable because the video already says what the audio says, then adding audio does not help — you now have more of the same duplication. Take the redundancy out of the video instead, and the audio suddenly has to carry something.

Removing is faster, and it teaches you what the principle actually is.

Module 4 vocabulary

Term Means
Removal test Delete one medium. If nothing breaks, the media were assembled rather than integrated
Integrated The media are interdependent — removing one breaks the piece
Assembled Media merely co-present. The failing state the removal test detects

Three terms, and this week is entirely the distinction between the last two. All are in the course glossary with their barred substitutes — read that column before you write your analysis.

Practise

Run what breaks? before the session. Work out which medium is doing a job only it can do, predict what a deletion costs, and diagnose failures in pieces that are not yours.

Round 3 is the one to do properly. It is diagnosis, which is what the written analysis is scored on — and it is much easier on someone else's work than on your own, which is exactly why to practise it there first.

Check yourself before the session

  1. State the removal test in one sentence.
  2. What is the difference between integrated and assembled?
  3. Name one thing audio can carry that a photograph cannot, and one thing a photograph can do that video cannot.
  4. Why does redundancy feel like good work while you are making it?
  5. Why does a correctly diagnosed failure score better than an untested claim?
  6. Your video is removable. What is the first thing you should consider changing — and it is not the video?

If you cannot answer 5, re-read section 6. It is what will stop you protecting a piece that needs testing.

What happens in the session

You take your photograph, your audio, and your video, and build one piece from them. Before you build, you write down what job each medium is doing. Afterwards you delete each one in turn and record what broke.

Then you swap with a partner, who deletes without sentiment. They will be less kind to your piece than you are, which is the point.

Full detail in the lab sheet, the removal test sheet, and the assignment.

This is week 15's dry run. Outcome 15.1 asks you to integrate three media into a landing page that passes the removal test — the same sentence, at full scale, with your whole grade attached. Better to find out here.
