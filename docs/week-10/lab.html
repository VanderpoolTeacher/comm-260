The knowledge check also happens in this session, and it is scored. It is a separate thing from this lab.

What you are doing

Taking one provided set of material and structuring it twice — once linear, once non-linear — then presenting both.

You are not using your own work, and that is deliberate. A student ordering their own photographs argues about the photographs. With material that is not yours, the only thing left to argue about is the order, which is the entire subject of this week.

You are not adding anything. Same items, both times. The constraint is what isolates structure as the variable.

Part 1 · Look at what you have been given

Eight to twelve items. Read them, look at them, and get a sense of what is there before you order anything.

There is no correct order. The set has been built so that it can be arranged in more than one defensible way — by time, by theme, by scale. If you find yourself hunting for the intended answer, stop; there isn't one.

Part 2 · Structure it linearly

One fixed order, start to finish.

Then write one sentence on what the order is doing:

  • What are you withholding, and until when?
  • What is the ending building to?
  • What does item 3 depend on the viewer having seen in item 2?

If you cannot answer any of those, you have arranged the items rather than structured them.

Part 3 · Restructure it non-linearly

Same items. No additions. The audience now chooses the path.

This can be done on paper — a map of entry points and the connections between them. You do not need to build anything.

Two questions to hold yourself to:

  1. How many entry points does it have? If the answer is one, you have made a linear piece with a menu.
  2. Does any item assume another was seen first? If yes, that item does not work cold — and every item has to work cold.

Part 4 · Present both

Walk the room through your two versions and say what changed.

Not what you did — what changed for the audience.

Part 5 · The cost question

You will be asked this about the non-linear version, and then about the linear one:

What did it lose?

You will have the gain ready. Everyone does. The question is the other half.

A cost is something you would rather not have paid. If what you say sounds like a feature, it is not a cost yet.

Not yet a cost A cost
"You lose control, which empowers the audience." "I can't guarantee anyone sees the first image before the second, and the comparison was the whole point."
"It's less linear, so it's more flexible." "Someone can land on the weakest item first and never see anything else."

Part 6 · Choose one, and defend it

Pick the version you would actually publish. Then defend it against the one you rejected.

The defence has to engage with the alternative. "I chose linear because it has a strong build" is praise for your choice. "I chose linear because the reversal at the end is the only reason this set is interesting, and non-linear would have let people arrive there first" engages with what you gave up.

That difference is outcome 10.4, and it is the whole of it.

Before you leave

You need all four for the assignment. Reconstructing them afterwards from memory is where most of the marks are lost.

What you hand in

See the assignment.

A written comparison — what changed, what was gained, what was lost, and which you chose.
