Week 10 · Linear and non-linear storytelling — Lesson

COMM 260 · Introduction to Digital Media Production

Read this before the session. About 25 minutes.

Three things before you arrive: review weeks 7–9 against the posted sheet, visit the two provided pieces — one linear, one non-linear — and write your own one-sentence definition of each.

Write those definitions before you read section 1 below if you can. Outcome 10.1 is define linear and non-linear structure in your own words, and a definition drafted cold is more honest than one written after being told the answer.

1 · The whole concept, in one question

Who decides what comes next — you, or them?

Linear Told in one fixed order, start to finish. You decide the order.
Non-linear The audience chooses the path through it. They decide.

That is it. Everything else in this week is consequences of that one difference.

2 · Four words that are not synonyms

You will reach for all of these. The glossary bars them, and the reason is that each one quietly swaps the concept for something else.

You will say It actually means Why it is not the same
Interactive The audience can act on it A film with a menu is interactive and entirely linear. Clicking play is not choosing a path
Branching One structure among several Branching is a way of being non-linear. A reference work with no branches at all is also non-linear
Random access You can reach any part directly A description of the technology, not the telling
Modular Built in parts A linear piece can be modular. The modules are still in one order

And the reverse trap, which is subtler:

Chronological is not the same as linear.

A piece can jump around in time and still be entirely linear — the order is fixed, it is just not time-order. Every flashback you have ever seen is linear.

And a piece can be strictly chronological and non-linear, if the audience can enter it at any point.

Linear is about whether the order is fixed. Not about whether the order is time.

3 · What each one buys, and what it charges

Outcome 10.3 is explicit: state what each structure makes possible and what it costs. Both halves, every time.

Naming the gain is easy and you will do it automatically. The cost is the half being assessed.

Linear

Buys you Charges you
A build. You know what the audience has already seen, so you can withhold something and then reveal it Their attention has to be earned continuously. If they leave at 40 seconds, everything after 40 seconds did not happen
Control over sequence, pace, and contrast No relevance to the individual. Everyone gets the same thing in the same order

The build is the thing. Suspense, a punchline, a reversal, an argument that lands because of what came before it — all of these need to know what the audience has already seen. Linear is the only structure that knows.

Non-linear

Buys you Charges you
Agency. People go to what they care about The build. You cannot rely on anything having been seen first
Relevance — different audiences get different routes Nothing can depend on anything. Every piece has to work cold
Depth without length. Nobody has to read the parts they do not need You lose the ability to withhold, and therefore to reveal

A piece whose entire effect is a reversal in the last thirty seconds cannot survive an audience that arrives there first.

That sentence is the week. It is also the reason a reference work is better non-linear and a joke is not.

4 · The cost answered with a gain

This is the single most common failure in this week's assessment, and it is worth recognising in advance because you will do it.

Asked what non-linear structure costs, students write something like:

"You lose control over the order — which is good, because the audience gets to choose what matters to them."

That is the gain, restated. The sentence names a loss and then immediately converts it into a benefit, so nothing has actually been given up.

A real cost is one you would rather not pay:

Not a cost A cost
"You lose control, which empowers the audience." "I cannot set anything up. The strongest thing I have is a comparison between two images, and I have no way to guarantee anyone sees the first one before the second."
"It is less linear, so it is more flexible." "Someone can land on my weakest page first and never see anything else."

If your stated cost sounds like a feature, it is not a cost.

5 · Your landing page is a structural decision

Week 15 asks you to justify the structure of your landing page, naming both gains and costs. That is outcome 15.2, and it is this week applied to the biggest thing you will make in this course.

Here is the thing worth noticing now: a landing page is one of the few genuinely non-linear objects most people ever build, and almost everybody builds one without ever noticing they made a choice.

Someone arrives at your page. Do they scroll it top to bottom in the order you set? Then you built something linear and put it on a web page. Can they jump straight to the video and never see the photographs? Then you built something non-linear — and if your positioning statement was supposed to frame everything else, it is not framing anything for that person.

Neither is wrong. Not noticing is.

Practise

Run gain or cost? before the session. Sort structures, catch the barred substitutes, and tell a real cost from a gain wearing a cost's clothes. Unlimited attempts, no marks.

Round 3 is the one to do properly. It is gain-versus-cost, which is what the written comparison is actually scored on.

Check yourself before the session

  1. In one sentence each, and in your own words: linear, and non-linear.
  2. Why is interactive not a synonym for non-linear?
  3. Can a piece be non-chronological and still linear? How?
  4. What does linear structure buy you that non-linear cannot?
  5. What makes a stated cost a real cost rather than a gain in disguise?
  6. Is a landing page linear or non-linear — and what does your answer depend on?

If you cannot answer 5, re-read section 4. It is what the written comparison is graded on.

What happens in the session

You take one provided set of material, structure it linearly, then restructure the same material non-linearly, and present both. Then you choose one and defend it against the version you rejected.

And then the knowledge check — the only written test in this course, covering weeks 7 through 10. Its exact scope is on the posted review sheet, and the sheet is precise on purpose: it is there so you can study the right things rather than everything.
