Week 7 · Camera and shot grammar — Lesson

COMM 260 · Introduction to Digital Media Production

Read this before the session. About 20 minutes.

Book a camera and a tripod before the lab, and watch the dialogue-free sequence you have been given. Write down every shot in order and what size you think it is. You will get the terminology wrong. That is not the point of the exercise.

You have spent six weeks learning to look and six days learning to listen. Video is both at once, and it is the last medium in this course for exactly that reason.

1 · Why video comes last

A video piece is photography that moves with audio attached.

That sentence is not a slogan — it is why this module sits at the end. Everything you meet in a video shoot, you have already met on its own: the frame in weeks 2 and 3, exposure in week 2, sound in weeks 5 and 6. A student who meets framing, exposure, and sound for the first time inside a video shoot meets all three badly, because a shoot gives you no time to think about any of them.

And video takes something away. Photography let you shoot in auto and fix it later. Audio let you set gain once and leave it. Video gives you neither — every frame is a new chance to be wrong, and there is no auto mode deep enough to hide in.

In week 2, manual mode, white balance, and ISO were surveyed: named and explained so the words existed, and explicitly not assessed. This is the week they stop being surveyed. If you treated week 2 as something to skim, you find out here.

2 · Every shot size has a job

A shot size describes how much of the world the frame contains. That is the easy half. The half that matters is what each size does to a viewer, because outcome 7.2 is name shot sizes and state what each one does for a viewer — the naming is worth nothing on its own.

Size Its job
Wide Tells you where you are
Medium wide Tells you who is here
Medium Tells you what they are doing
Medium close Tells you how they feel
Close-up Tells you what to feel

The medium shot is where most amateur footage lives, and the reason is not that it is right. It is that it is the least decision — far enough away to include everything, close enough to see faces, safe. A sequence shot entirely in mediums communicates almost nothing, because nothing ever changes.

This is the same idea as week 1's default: something the maker never questioned. A medium shot can absolutely be a deliberate choice. Five of them in a row is a default.

3 · Frame rate and shutter are not the same number

These two get confused for the first hour of every video course ever taught, and the confusion is understandable — they are both numbers, they are set in different menus, and one is usually written with a slash.

Is Governs
Frame rate How many images per second How motion is sampled — how many slices of it you keep
Shutter How long each image is exposed How motion looks within one frame — smeared, or frozen

Shutter is the one that changes how the picture feels, and you will see it demonstrated. A shutter left open too long gives you motion blur so heavy that movement turns to mush. A shutter closed too fast freezes every frame individually, and played back at speed the result stutters — each frame is sharp, and the gaps between them become visible.

Neither is a mistake in itself. There are things you might want either one for. What is not available to you is not having decided.

Rule versus reason

Outcome 7.1 is configure a camera for video — frame rate, shutter, resolution — and say why each value. That last clause is the whole outcome, and it is where this week is passed or failed.

A rule A reason
"Because it's the standard." "Because I want the motion to look natural rather than stuttered."
"Because that's what the class is set to." "Because the subject moves fast and I want each frame sharp enough to hold on."
"Because 1/50 goes with 25." "Because doubling the frame rate is roughly how long the eye expects each frame to be open, and I wanted it to look ordinary."

A rule tells me you read something. A reason tells me you decided something. In the lab you will be asked why that value, out loud, for each of the three settings — and a rule will be sent back for a reason.

4 · Resolution, which is the quick one

Resolution is how many pixels make up each image. It is the least interesting of the three settings and should take you the least time.

It matters mostly downstream: in week 9 you export to a stated specification, and the sequence you cut in has to match the footage you shot. Footage shot at a resolution nobody wrote down is where that goes wrong.

Set it deliberately. Write it down. Move on.

5 · Coverage

Coverage is shooting the same moment from more than one size.

It does two things, and the second is the one students miss.

  1. It gives the edit options. A moment covered once can only be cut one way.
  2. The shift between sizes is itself meaning. Cutting from a wide to a close-up says look at this. Cutting the other way says and here is where that was happening. The change carries an argument even when nothing in the world changed.

That second point is why this week is called shot grammar rather than shot sizes. A sentence is not a pile of words.

6 · Movement, and restraint

Given a camera, people move it. Almost always.

Outcome 7.4 is decide when camera movement serves the shot and when it only announces itself, and the honest version of that is: most student camera movement is the second kind. The move happens, the viewer notices the camera, and nothing has been revealed.

A move that serves the shot reveals something that was not available before it. The frame arrives somewhere. Something comes into view that you could not see, or the relationship between two things becomes clear because the camera travelled between them.

A move that announces itself just changes where the camera is pointing, slowly, while you watch it happen.

The default position of this course: a locked-off tripod shot is a decision. A drifting handheld shot usually is not.

That is a starting position, not a law. Argue with it — but argue with it by naming what your move revealed. This is week 1's distinction again, applied to a camera: a deliberate choice is one where doing otherwise was genuinely available to you, and a default is one you never questioned.

You are required to use the tripod for at least three of your five shots, and the reason is that you cannot decide between locked-off and moving if you have never done the first one.

7 · A sequence that reads

Outcome 7.3 is shoot a sequence that reads without dialogue, and the test is brutally simple: someone who was not there watches it with the sound off and can say what happened.

They will not have your explanation. That is the point. If a sequence needs you standing next to it saying "so she's making coffee, and then she—", it did not read, and the explanation is the evidence.

Five shots. No dialogue, no titles. What you are handing in is unedited, in your intended order — because outcome 7.3 asks whether the shots were planned to read, not whether an edit rescued them afterwards.

Practise

Run size and its job before the session. Match sizes to what they do, tell a rule from a reason, and judge whether a move revealed anything. Unlimited attempts, no marks.

Round 2 is the one to do properly. It is rule-versus-reason, and it is the exact question you will be asked out loud in the lab.

Check yourself before the session

  1. What does a wide shot do for a viewer that a close-up cannot?
  2. What is the difference between frame rate and shutter, in one sentence each?
  3. Why is a sequence of five medium shots a problem?
  4. What makes an answer a reason rather than a rule?
  5. What does a camera move have to do to earn its place?
  6. Why are you handing in unedited footage rather than an edit?

If you cannot answer 4, re-read section 3. It is the question you will be asked out loud, three times, in the lab.

What happens in the session

Your instructor watches each of you configure a camera unaided, before anyone shoots. Then you shoot a five-shot sequence, and some of them are screened to the room with the sound off.

Full detail in the lab sheet and the assignment.

This is the only time in the course a video camera is observed. There is no later week that replaces it.
