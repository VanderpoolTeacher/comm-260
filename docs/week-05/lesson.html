COMM 260 · Introduction to Digital Media Production

Read this before the session. About 20 minutes.

Book your equipment before the lab. This is the first week you have to, and the week does not work if you arrive empty-handed.

1 · Why audio is the hard one

You have spent four weeks learning to look. Everything you have been assessed on so far has been visible — you could see the crop, see the focus, see whether the light landed.

Audio gives you none of that.

You cannot look at a waveform and know whether it sounds right. A recording can look perfect on a meter and be unusable. And the problems that ruin it — a room that rings, a microphone in the wrong place, a level set too low — are invisible until you listen, and often invisible until you listen on something better than laptop speakers.

That is why this module exists on its own, and why it comes before video. Put audio next to picture and everyone spends their time on the picture.

2 · Naming what is wrong

In the session you will hear two recordings of the same thing — one well recorded, one badly. You will be asked what is wrong with the bad one before anyone gives you the words.

You will say things like echoey · muffled · far away · tinny. Those are the right instincts and the wrong vocabulary.

Here are the words:

You hear It is
Echoey, boxy Room reflection — sound bouncing off hard surfaces back into the microphone
Far away, thin Wrong mic distance — the microphone was too far from the source
Hissy, muffled Gain set too low and lifted afterwards — the noise came up with the voice

You cannot catch a problem you cannot name. That is outcome 5.3's precondition, and it is the whole reason the session opens this way.

3 · Three microphones, three situations

Do not learn these as a taxonomy. Learn them as answers to situations.

Situation Answer Why
A seated interview Lavalier Close, consistent, stays out of frame
A subject you cannot approach Shotgun Rejects sound from the sides; picks up what it is pointed at
A noisy room where you can get close Handheld Robust, forgiving of handling, easy to move

Outcome 5.1 says choose a microphone that suits a stated recording situation — so the situation comes first and the microphone follows.

The shotgun will surprise you. It looks the most professional and it is the least forgiving: point it slightly wrong, or put it slightly too far away, and it sounds worse than the cheap option.

4 · Gain staging — the hardest thing here

Gain is how much the recorder amplifies what the microphone hears. Set it once, before you record, by watching the meter while your subject talks normally.

The target

Speech peaks between −12 and −6 dBFS

That is the window. Not an average — the peaks.

The two ways it goes wrong

Too hot. The signal exceeds 0 and clips. The waveform flattens at the top and the sound distorts. Obvious, and unfixable.

Too quiet. This is the one nobody believes. You record at −40, it sounds fine and quiet, and you think you will just turn it up later.

When you turn it up, everything comes up with it. The hiss of the preamp. The air conditioning. The hum in the building. Those were always there, sitting below the voice — lifting the voice lifts them too, and now they are audible.

Quiet feels safer than clipped. It is not.

Both are recoverable in the ear. Neither is recoverable in the file.

You will hear this demonstrated. It is the moment the week turns.

5 · Monitoring is a habit, not a fact

Headphones on before you record. Not after.

That sentence is the whole of outcome 5.3. Monitoring is not a step you complete — it is a state you stay in while recording, so that when something goes wrong you hear it at the moment it happens rather than at home.

Closed-back headphones are required. Not earbuds, not open-back, not laptop speakers. You are listening for a noise floor and a room, and neither survives on anything else.

The test that matters

Record a test. Listen back. Change something.

A test you listened to and changed nothing about is not monitoring — it is a ritual. If you genuinely heard nothing to adjust, say what you were listening for.

6 · Room tone

Thirty seconds of the room, with nobody speaking, recorded at every location.

It is not silence. Every room has a sound — air handling, traffic, the building itself — and that sound is specific to that room at that moment.

You will be asked what it is for. You do not need the whole answer this week.

You will feel it next week, when you cut two takes together and hear the room vanish for a moment at the join. Then it will be obvious. For now: capture it, and hold the question.

It will feel much longer than thirty seconds and you will want to cut it short. Do not.

Module 3 vocabulary

Term Means
Gain How much the recorder amplifies what the microphone hears
dBFS The scale the meter uses. 0 is the ceiling; everything usable is below it
Clipping Signal exceeding 0 dBFS. Distorted, and unfixable
Noise floor The constant low-level sound underneath everything. Lifting a quiet recording lifts this too
Room tone Thirty seconds of a room with nobody speaking. Specific to that room, that day
Phantom power Power sent to a microphone that needs it. Either on, or the microphone is silent
Monitoring Listening on headphones while recording, to catch problems as they happen
Lavalier · Shotgun · Handheld Three microphone types, each suited to a stated situation

All are in the course glossary. Room tone is distinct from silence — silence is a choice, room tone is a recording.

Check yourself before the session

  1. Why can you not tell whether a recording is good by looking at it?
  2. What are the three named faults you will hear in the bad recording?
  3. What is the gain target, in dBFS?
  4. Why is recording too quiet worse than it sounds?
  5. When do the headphones go on?
  6. What is room tone, and how is it different from silence?

If you cannot answer 4, re-read section 4. It is the thing the week turns on.

Practise

Run mic for the situation before the session — match microphones to situations and diagnose faults by ear. Unlimited attempts, no marks.

What happens in the session

You record one subject three ways, and your instructor watches each of you set up unaided. Full detail in the lab sheet and the assignment.

Keep the files. Week 6 builds a piece from them.
