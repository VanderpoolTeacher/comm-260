Week 9 · Non-linear editing — Lesson

COMM 260 · Introduction to Digital Media Production

Read this before the session. About 20 minutes.

Three things before you arrive: bring your team's week 8 footage and the separately recorded audio, on storage you control; open the files and confirm they play; and read the posted export specification. You are expected to arrive knowing what the numbers are. This week is where you find out what they mean.

You shot it as a team. You cut it alone. Three people editing the same material will produce three different pieces, and that difference is the first honest evidence of what an edit actually is.

1 · The sequence has to match the footage

Outcome 9.1 is create a project and sequence that match your footage.

When you start a project, the editing software asks — or worse, quietly assumes — what shape the pictures are: how many pixels, how many frames per second. That is the sequence, and if it does not match what your camera recorded, you will see it immediately. The picture is scaled, or letterboxed, or the motion plays back oddly.

This is the easiest thing in the week to get right and the most common thing to get wrong, because getting it wrong requires no action at all. You accept a default preset, the software does not complain, and the mismatch shows up in playback.

The fix is to know what you shot. That was week 7 — frame rate, shutter, resolution, and write it down. This is the week that instruction gets cashed.

A note on the word. In week 7, a sequence was a run of shots that reads as one piece of action. The editing software uses the same word for the timeline you cut in. This course knows about the collision and has not settled it yet — see the glossary. Say which one you mean.

2 · The dull outcome that costs the most

Outcome 9.2 is organise media so the project can be reopened in six months and still work.

It is the least interesting thing in this module. It is also the one that decides whether you have a final project.

Here is the failure, and it is not dramatic. Your footage is on a phone. Your audio is in a downloads folder. There is an image on the desktop. The project opens today, because every one of those paths still exists on this machine, on this afternoon. Next week you sit at a different workstation and the project opens to a wall of red.

Three habits prevent all of it:

Do Because
Put every piece of media in one place before importing anything The project stores where things are. If where things are is four places, it stores four fragile answers
Name bins for what is in them — not Bin 1, Bin 2 You are naming these for the version of you that opens this in week 14 and remembers nothing
Keep the project file with its media A project file separated from its media is a list of instructions about files that are not there

Week 14 is where the bill arrives. The final project's video piece is submitted with its project file — and a project file that cannot be opened evidences nothing at all. Not "loses a few marks." Evidences nothing.

Do this before you start cutting. Once you have finished an edit, nobody reorganises it properly, including you.

3 · Sync is a problem before it is a technique

In week 8 you recorded audio separately, and not into the camera. The reason was made in week 5: the microphone on a camera is in the wrong place — it is where the camera is, and the camera is not where the sound is.

The cost of that decision arrives now. Picture and sound came back as two files, and they have to be put back together.

Outcome 9.3 is sync separately recorded audio to video. Whatever method you are shown, one rule holds:

Verify on headphones, not by eye.

Students drag the audio until the waveform looks right and stop there. Speech that is a few frames out does not look wrong. It sounds wrong immediately — slightly uncanny, like a badly dubbed film, and you cannot un-notice it once you hear it.

4 · Cutting on action

Outcome 9.4 is cut on action, and it is the week's craft outcome.

A cut placed between movements is the safe one. Someone finishes reaching for the door, you cut, the next shot begins. It works, and the audience sees the cut.

A cut placed in the middle of the movement is the one you do not see. The hand starts to reach in the wide shot and finishes reaching in the close. The eye is following the motion, so it follows the motion across the join — and the join goes unnoticed.

Cut between movements Cut on action
Safe, and visible Invisible, and harder
The audience notices the edit The audience notices the action
What you will do by default What outcome 9.4 asks for

You will see both, on the same two shots, in the session. It is a difference you cannot argue with once you have watched it.

Two of your cuts must be on action, and you have to be able to point at them. An accidental one does not evidence the outcome.

5 · Sixty seconds is the point

The finished piece is 60 seconds, and the limit is not an administrative convenience.

You shot more than sixty seconds. The limit forces you to choose, and choosing is where your week 8 shot list either helped or did not. A team whose list was derived from a treatment has an argument to select against. A team whose list was invented has a pile of shots and no reason to prefer any of them.

That is the week 8 lesson arriving one week later, which is roughly how long it takes for planning to prove itself.

6 · Export, and what the numbers mean

Outcome 9.5 is export to a stated specification and say what the settings mean. Both halves.

The specification is posted. Meeting it is checkable — you either did or you did not. What the outcome actually asks for is the second half:

A student who followed instructions A student who understands
"1080p." "1080p because the footage is 1080p, and upscaling invents detail that was never recorded."
"H.264." "H.264 because it plays everywhere without anyone installing anything."
"10 Mbps." "10 Mbps because below that this much movement starts to break up in the shadows."

And then verify it plays somewhere that is not the machine you made it on. An export that only opens inside your editing software is not an export. It is a file that happens to exist.

Module 4 vocabulary

This week's terms. The camera terms from week 7 and the planning terms from week 8 are assumed.

Term Means
Media management Organising footage so the project can be reopened later and still work
Sync Aligning separately recorded audio to picture
Cutting on action Cutting mid-movement so the action carries across the join
Export specification The stated format, resolution, and settings a finished piece must be delivered in
Project file The editing application's session file. Submitted, not just the video

All are in the course glossary. Project file is barred from being called a timeline, a session, or a working file.

Practise

Run six months from now before the session. Judge whether a project will still open, match footage to sequence settings, and say what an export number does. Unlimited attempts, no marks.

Round 2 is the one to do properly — it is media management, which is the outcome you will be tempted to skip and the one that costs you a final project.

Check yourself before the session

  1. What is a sequence setting for, and how would you know yours was wrong?
  2. Name the three habits that keep a project openable.
  3. Why verify sync on headphones rather than by eye?
  4. What is the difference between a cut on action and a cut between movements?
  5. Why is the piece limited to 60 seconds?
  6. What does it mean to say what an export setting means, as opposed to meeting the specification?

If you cannot answer 4, re-read section 4. It is the week's only craft outcome.

What happens in the session

Your instructor watches each of you create a project and sequence unaided, before anyone starts cutting. Then you sync, cut to sixty seconds, export to the specification, and check the file plays on another machine.
