Week 6 · Editing and sound design — Lesson

COMM 260 · Introduction to Digital Media Production

Read this before the session. About 20 minutes.

Bring your week 5 recordings — all three, plus your room tone. Bring closed-back headphones.

1 · What you are proving

That your edit is better than what you started with.

Not "sounds good." Better than the raw — which is why you submit both. The claim becomes checkable instead of asserted, and that is outcome 6.4.

It also means an edit that is merely clean does not do very much. Cleaning is expected. Shaping is the work.

2 · Cutting for clarity

Removing false starts, stumbles, long breaths, and the ums that get in the way.

Easy to do. Easy to overdo.

Outcome 6.1 has a constraint built into it: without making it sound processed. Speech cut too tight stops sounding like a person thinking. It becomes uncanny before it becomes clear, and listeners notice something is wrong without being able to say what.

The usual cause is that every breath is gone. People breathe. Take out all the breathing and you have made a machine.

You will hear this demonstrated. Nobody can name it until it is named for them.

3 · Cutting for meaning

This is the outcome that separates the week from a technical exercise.

A structural cut changes what the piece is about:

  • Moving the strongest line to the front, so it frames everything after it
  • Cutting an explanation so the listener works it out themselves
  • Ending before the resolution, so the question stays open

Outcome 6.2 is make a structural cut that changes how the piece lands, and say why.

The second half is the assessment. A student who reorders material and cannot say what the new order does has not met the outcome — they have shuffled.

And as everywhere in this course: name the cost. Moving the strongest line to the front gives you a hook and spends your best material immediately. That is a real trade and you should be able to state it.

4 · Noise reduction, and its limits

You will reach for this first. Do not.

Noise reduction pushed hard makes speech swirl — a watery, underwater quality around the edges of words. It is the most common failure in the submitted piece, every year.

Here is the part that matters:

The fix was in week 5, at the microphone.

That is the module's whole argument in one sentence. A quiet recording lifted in the edit brings the noise floor up with it — you heard that demonstrated last week — and no amount of processing puts it back.

Use the smallest amount that works. Last, and least.

5 · Music under speech

Outcome 6.3: at levels that serve both.

Loud enough to be felt. Quiet enough that no word is lost.

There is no number. It is set by listening — on headphones, then checked on speakers, because a bed that sits nicely on headphones often disappears or dominates in a room.

Music does something specific: it tells the listener how to feel about what they are hearing, before the words get there. That is powerful and it is easy to abuse. If your piece needs music to be interesting, the problem is the edit.

6 · Room tone — the answer

Last week you were asked what room tone is for and told you would find out.

Here it is.

Cut two takes together. At the join, the room's sound stops for an instant and starts again — a tiny hole in the world. You may not name it, but you hear it, and the cut announces itself.

Lay room tone underneath the join and the cut vanishes. The room continues; only the voice changed.

That is what it is for. It is not silence — silence is a choice, room tone is a recording.

Module 3 vocabulary — additions

Term Means
Structural cut A cut that changes what the piece is about, not just its length
Noise reduction Processing that removes constant background sound. Pushed hard, it makes speech swirl
Bed Music running underneath speech
Raw sources Your unedited originals, kept and submitted

Raw sources bars originals, masters, source files, and unedited in the glossary. All are in the course glossary.

Check yourself before the session

  1. What are you actually proving this week, and how is it checkable?
  2. What does over-cut speech sound like, and what is usually missing?
  3. What makes a cut structural rather than just tidy?
  4. Where was the real fix for a noisy recording?
  5. What is room tone for?
  6. Why do you submit the raw sources as well as the edit?

If you cannot answer 4, re-read section 4. It is the module's argument.

What happens in the session

You build a 60-second piece from your week 5 recordings. Full detail in the lab sheet and the assignment.

Submit the edit and the raw sources. Both — the same pairing the final project requires in week 13, which is exactly why it is required here first.
