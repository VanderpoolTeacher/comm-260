What you are doing

Producing two components in one phase — twelve photographs and a two-to-three-minute audio piece — plus crewing on somebody else's production.

This is the phase people underestimate. Week 3 asked for six images. Week 6 asked for sixty seconds. This asks for both, at double the scale, in one week.

Part 1 · Read your guide against your intent — in the room, before anything else

Put them beside each other and read them as if somebody else wrote them.

Find the collision. There usually is one, and it is invisible while you are writing either document because both felt right at the time.

Your guide says Your intent says
f/2.8 or wider, every portrait the room has to be legible Cannot both hold
Available light only, no flash shot in the workshop after dark Cannot both hold
Subject on a third, horizon never centred a set about symmetry Cannot both hold

If they are compatible, say so and move on. Not every pair fights, and a guide rewritten to fix a conflict that was not there has lost a constraint for nothing.

Part 2 · Resolve it, and record it

Two legitimate options. One that is not.

Revise the guide Revise the intent Ignore one and hope
Your rule was too broad Your intent needed narrowing

Name the exception. Do not delete the rule.

"Shallow depth — f/2.8 or wider — for close portraits. f/8 or narrower where the environment is the subject."

That keeps the constraint and admits one case. Deleting the aperture line entirely leaves your guide saying nothing about aperture at all — and twelve photographs with nothing to be checked against.

Then write down what the revision cost you. In the example above: the guide is now more permissive, so two images that look nothing alike can both comply. That is a real loss and you should know you paid it.

Record the revision and date it.

A revision dated before the shoot is a decision. A revision dated after it is an alibi — unless it says so plainly, in which case it is a decision too.

Revising after shooting is completely legitimate if you write "discovered during the shoot that…". Quietly editing the guide to match what you captured is not. Keep the old version.

Part 3 · The photography set — 12 images

Both halves at once. Outcome 13.1 is meet your technical specs and serve a stated intent, and you can pass one and fail the other.

  • Creative intent written before shooting. As in week 3.
  • Twelve images that comply with your own guide — the resolved version.
  • Capture settings recorded at the time, and a tools statement with reasons.
  • It has to read as one body of work. Week 3's outcome 3.4 still applies. Twelve is not six, twice — selecting down happens once, across the whole set.

Part 4 · The audio piece — 2–3 minutes

Weeks 5 and 6, at two to three times the length.

  • Book the quiet space early. It is bookable and it runs out, and the most common failure of this phase is a piece recorded at the last minute in a room that rings.
  • Mic chosen for the situation. Gain in the −12 to −6 dBFS window. Headphones on before you record. Room tone captured.
  • Cut for clarity and for meaning, with a structural decision you can name.
  • To your style guide's audio rules — levels, and whether music sits under speech.
  • Submit the edited piece and your raw sources. Both. Without the raw, "better than the raw" is not checkable, and unassessable is worse than imperfect.

Part 5 · Crewing — one session, this phase

On a classmate's production, in a support role.

  • Get the producer's signature on the day. A log signed retroactively in week 15 is not evidence.
  • Log what you were responsible for, one thing that went wrong, and how it was handled.
  • Check the constraint: three phases, three different producers. If phase 1 was the same person, change one now — there is only one phase left after this.

Component G is 10% of the project. The same weight as your entire photography set.
