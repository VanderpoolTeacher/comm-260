Week 1 · Visual literacy — Lesson

COMM 260 · Introduction to Digital Media Production

Read this before the session. About 25 minutes.

The photographs in this lesson were generated, and are labelled as such. They are here to show a mechanism — what an edge excludes, what directs the eye — not to be read for a maker's intent. You will be asked to read real work, credited, from week 2 onward.

You are not making anything this week. You are learning to say what other people made — because for the next fourteen weeks you will be asked to defend your own decisions, and you cannot defend a choice you cannot name.

1 · The problem this course starts with

Show someone a photograph they like and ask why. You will hear:

It's nice. The colours. It feels calm. The lighting is good.

Every one of those is a preference. None of them says what the photographer did.

That gap is the whole reason this week exists. A preference is a fact about you. A named choice is a fact about the work — and it is the only one of the two that can be discussed, taught, defended, or graded.

Preference Named choice
"The background is blurry" "The shallow depth of field isolates him from the crowd"
"It's really quiet in the middle" "The four-second pause after she answers makes you sit with it"
"The camera is really close" "The close-up arrives as she stops talking, so you read her face instead of her words"

The left column says what you noticed. The right says what the maker did and what it accomplished.

The right column is what you are graded on — this week, and in week 15, and in every week between.

A photograph with one legible decision in it. Say what the maker did, and what it accomplished — not whether you like it. Generated image.

2 · Everything in the frame was put there

A photograph has an edge. Everything inside it was placed there or allowed to remain. Everything outside it was excluded.

Exclusion is the decision nobody notices, because absence does not draw attention to itself. Nothing in an image says "there was a car park just out of shot."

When you look at an image, ask two questions:

  1. What is just outside the edge?
  2. Why is the edge there?

Then imagine the frame opening up by a metre in every direction. Usually the subject stops being the only thing, the context arrives, and the image becomes about a situation rather than a person.

That change is what the crop bought. Cropping in the camera is a decision about what the picture is about — not a tidying-up you do afterwards.

The same moment, framed two ways. The tight one is about a person; the wide one is about a street in the rain. Nothing changed but the edge. Generated image.

3 · Three ways to tell the eye where to go

Light, focus, and colour all do one job: they direct attention.

How it directs
Focus The sharp thing is read first; soft things recede
Light The bright thing is read first in a dim frame
Colour The saturated thing is read first in a muted frame

Read those three descriptions again and notice the second half of each. Sharp against soft. Bright in a dim frame. Saturated in a muted one. Nothing here works on its own — each one depends on a contrast with what surrounds it. Put a bright subject in an equally bright frame and it stops reading first, without anything about the subject having changed.

Each time you see one of these working, ask: what would I look at if this were removed?

Focus, light, colour — the same scene with a different one doing the work each time. Generated image.

Here focus is not the variable — everything is sharp. Light is doing the work. Generated image.

Learn these as functions before you learn them as techniques. A student who learns shallow depth of field as a technique reaches for it constantly. A student who learns it as one of several ways to make the subject read first chooses between them.

Coming in week 2: this has a name — subject emphasis — and there are five means of it, contrast among them. You will be asked which one is doing the most work in a given image. You do not need that yet. Notice the effect now; name it precisely next week.

4 · The ear: pace and silence

Sound has the same problem as the frame, and it is harder, because you have no vocabulary for duration.

Play someone an audio piece and ask what they heard. They will list what was audible — the voice, the music, the room. They will not mention the gaps.

Silence is doing work, and it is invisible because nothing is there.

It works by contrast, exactly as the picture does — a pause is only a pause against the sound around it.

A pause after a difficult answer makes you sit with it. A rest before a phrase returns makes you lean in. Close those gaps — in speech or in music — and the piece feels rushed and slightly desperate, and most listeners cannot say why.

You will hear this demonstrated in the session: one passage played as recorded, then again with the pauses removed and nothing else changed.

Pace is the same idea across a whole piece. How long you are held on one thing before being moved to the next is a choice, in audio and in video, exactly as much as a crop is.

5 · Choice, or default?

Here is the hard one.

Not everything in a piece of media was decided. Some of it is just how it came out.

  • Grain in a photograph might be a deliberate texture — or the room was dark.
  • A handheld, drifting camera might be urgency — or there was no tripod.
  • Compressed, thin audio might be a phone-call effect — or it was recorded on a phone.

Beginners credit the maker with everything. Everything becomes intentional, and the analysis stops being useful.

Grain, handheld, low light. Deliberate texture, or a dark room and no tripod? Ask what it would have taken to do otherwise. Generated image.

The test question:

What would it have taken to do otherwise?

If doing otherwise would have been trivial and they did not, that is evidence of a decision. If doing otherwise would have required equipment, time, or access they plainly did not have, you are probably looking at a constraint, not a choice.

You are not being graded on guessing what anyone was thinking. You cannot know. You are being graded on whether you can tell a decision was made here from this is just how it came out — and on whether you can say what the alternative would have required.

6 · Every choice costs something

This is the part most people skip, and it is the part that separates a judgment from an opinion.

Every choice forecloses something else:

The choice What it buys What it costs
Shallow depth of field The subject is isolated and unmissable The room. You lose the context.
A tight crop Attention goes exactly where you want The situation. You lose the surroundings.
A long pause Weight, space to absorb something Pace. The piece slows.
Bright, even lighting Everything is legible Drama. Nothing is emphasised.

Naming what a choice accomplishes makes you observant. Naming what it cost makes you a producer.

When you write your assignment, the third part — what the alternative would have been and what it would have cost — is where most marks are lost. It is easy to skip and hard to fake, because naming a cost means committing to a claim about what the piece is for.

Module 1 vocabulary

These nine terms are Module 1's own. They are what week 1 teaches and what the assignment is marked against. All nine are in the course glossary, which you receive this week and which does not change for the rest of the term.

Term Means
Preference A statement about what you like. Says nothing about what the maker did, and is not evidence.
Named choice What the maker did, in course vocabulary, together with what it accomplished.
The frame The edge of the image — and therefore everything included and everything excluded.
Exclusion What was deliberately left outside the frame. A decision, and the one nobody notices.
Deliberate choice Something the maker decided, where doing otherwise was genuinely available.
Default Something never questioned, or that could not have been changed. Not a mistake.
Cost What a choice gave up. Every choice has one.
Pace How long you are held on one thing before being moved to the next — picture or sound.
Silence Sound deliberately withheld. Distinct from room tone, which you meet in week 5.

Check your words against the glossary before you submit. The Never use column is the part that makes it binding — those words are barred because they mean two things at once, and precision is most of what this assignment tests.

Words you will hear but do not own yet

You will hear contrast, shallow depth of field, close-up, wide shot, and room tone used in the session. They are not Module 1 vocabulary and they are not on this assignment. Each is owned by a later module — contrast and depth of field in week 2, shot sizes in week 7, room tone in week 5 — and you will be assessed on them there.

Contrast is the one you will hear most. It is the reason light, focus, and colour work at all, and week 2 gives it a proper definition as one of the five means of subject emphasis.

Notice what they do now. Name them precisely later.

Practise the vocabulary

Run the vocabulary match before the session. Three rounds on the nine terms above — match each term to what it does, then name what an image is doing, then rewrite a preference as a named choice.

Unlimited attempts, no marks. It is where being wrong is free.

Check yourself before the session

You should be able to answer these without looking back. They are not graded — they are how you find out whether the reading landed.

  1. What is the difference between a preference and a named choice?
  2. Why is exclusion the hardest decision to notice?
  3. Name the three things that direct the eye in an image, and what each does.
  4. What is the test question for telling a deliberate choice from a default?
  5. A photographer uses shallow depth of field to isolate a subject. What did it cost?
  6. Why does naming the cost matter more than naming the effect?

If you cannot answer 4 or 6, re-read sections 5 and 6. They are the two the assignment is built on.

What happens in the session

You will read three professional pieces — one photo essay, one audio piece, one short film — and name the choices you see in each. Full detail in the lab sheet.

You are not photographing anything, and you do not need a camera.

Then you submit three choices, one per piece. Full detail in the assignment.
