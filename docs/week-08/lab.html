Week 8 Lab — Team production

COMM 260 · Introduction to Digital Media Production

This happens in the room. Bring your team's booked equipment — camera, tripod, and a separate audio recorder with a microphone. Know which role you hold first.

Two of the three things you submit are your team's. One is yours alone — the role reflection, and it is the only part nobody else can write for you.

What you are doing

Planning and shooting a production in a team of three, rotating roles so that every one of you holds the producer role once.

This is the only shared-accountability work in this course. Everything else you make, you make alone.

Your instructor watches. They will not run it for you. If your team stalls, that is your team's problem to solve, and solving it is the thing being assessed.

Part 1 · Fill in the top of the record, before anything else

Open the team production record now, as a team.

Agree the rotation and write it down before you plan, let alone shoot. Three people, four roles — something doubles up, and you decide which.

Check that every name appears in the Producer column exactly once. Do that now. It is a thirty-second check that the entire week rests on.

Fill the rest of the record in as you go, not at the end. One completed in the last five minutes is a reconstruction and it reads like one.

Part 2 · Plan together, in the room, before anything is shot

In this order:

  1. Treatment — what the piece is, who it is for, what it is trying to do. Two or three paragraphs, no shot numbers. It has to rule something out, or it has decided nothing.
  2. Shot list — derived from the treatment. Not written separately.
  3. Storyboard — for the sequence that genuinely needs one, not the whole thing.

The derivation is the point. For every line on your shot list, be able to point at the sentence in the treatment that asked for it. A shot with no sentence behind it is a good shot from a piece you have not written — so either cut it, or change the treatment so that it asks for it. Both are honest. Keeping both and calling the list derived is not.

Storyboard a sequence, not the production. Pick the one where the shots are hard to hold in your head — a match across a cut, a reveal that depends on what came before, geography a viewer could get lost in. Boxes and stick figures carry the information. Nobody is assessing the drawing.

Part 3 · The four roles

Role You own
Director What the shot is
Camera The image
Sound The recording
Producer The day — the schedule, the equipment, whether it finishes

With three people and four roles, some rotations double up. You decided which in Part 1. Left unstated, the ambiguity resolves itself as nobody did sound — and sound is not decoration this week.

Part 4 · Everyone produces once

This is the requirement the week is built on.

When you are producer:

  • You call what happens next. When a shot is done, they look at you.
  • You keep the shoot on the schedule, and you decide what to drop when it slips.
  • You are the person the others are waiting on.

Producing is deciding what happens next. The visible work is camera and sound, and the most common mistake in this lab is holding the producer role and standing still because it looks like nobody needs you.

Two people idle is a scheduling failure, not a personality one. While the next shot is being set up, somebody could be getting room tone.

Write your decisions in the record as you make them. Not what happened — what you decided.

Part 5 · Record audio separately

Not into the camera.

Next week you sync separately recorded audio to picture. A team that records into the camera has deleted one of week 9's outcomes for all three of you, and it gets discovered next week, when it cannot be fixed.

Check a file during the shoot, not after. Confirm the audio is on the recorder.

Part 6 · Individual role reflection

Each of you writes your own, afterwards, naming what you were responsible for in each rotation and what you actually did.

Not a summary of the shoot. What you were accountable for, in the first person, including the rotation where you produced.

Detail in the assignment.

Before you leave

What you hand in

Team planning documents, footage and separate audio, and your individual role reflection.

Fall Break

This week loses a session to the break.

Plan the shoot around it, not through it. A shoot day with a four-day gap in the middle is not a shoot day — and keeping the day on track is exactly what you are being assessed on.

Check that your equipment booking covers the days nobody is on campus. A booking that lapses over the break is a producer's problem, and it is one you can solve now instead of then.
