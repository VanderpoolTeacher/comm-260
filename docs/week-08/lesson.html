Week 8 · Production planning and team production — Lesson

COMM 260 · Introduction to Digital Media Production

Read this before the session. About 25 minutes.

Three things before you arrive: read the example treatment and the shot list derived from it, know which role you hold first, and book your team's equipment. The booking is not preparation for outcome 8.3 — it is outcome 8.3, and it starts before the shoot rather than on the day.

Last week you found out that the camera decides nothing for you. This week you find out that on a real shoot, neither do you — not alone.

1 · The only week you are not working alone

Every other thing you make in this course, you make by yourself. Your photographs, your audio piece, your five shots, your landing page. Even the final project, which is four weeks long, is yours.

This week, three of you are accountable for one result.

That is not a scheduling convenience or a way to share out the equipment. It is the subject. Outcome 8.3 — schedule a shoot and run it — is the only place in this course where you are accountable for other people's time, and there is no later week where it comes back.

Which means something worth saying plainly: this is the one week where being quietly competent is not enough. If your team cannot say what you were responsible for, you have not evidenced outcome 8.5, no matter how much work you actually did.

2 · A treatment is an argument, not a description

A treatment is a short prose document that says what the piece is, who it is for, and what it is trying to do. Two or three paragraphs. No shot numbers, no technical detail.

The thing that makes it useful is that it commits to something.

A description A treatment
"A short video about the campus radio station." "A short video about how much work happens in a room nobody visits. We follow one presenter through a shift, and the piece is built to make the empty corridor outside feel as present as the studio."

The second one tells you what to shoot. It also tells you what not to shoot, which is the half people forget — a treatment that admits everything has decided nothing.

You will be able to tell whether you have written one by asking whether it rules anything out.

3 · The shot list comes from the treatment

Outcome 8.1 is write a treatment and build a shot list from it. Read that again. It is not two documents — it is one document and a derivation.

A shot list is every shot you intend to get, in an order you can shoot in, with the size and whatever else the shot needs.

The derivation is the skill. Each line on the list should be traceable to something the treatment committed to:

The treatment said So the list contains
"...a room nobody visits" Wide of the empty corridor. Wide of the empty studio before she arrives
"...one presenter through a shift" Medium of her setting up. Close on hands on the desk. Medium close during a link
"...the corridor as present as the studio" The corridor again, at the end, unchanged

Every one of those exists because a sentence in the treatment asked for it. Nothing in that list is there because it seemed like a good idea.

A shot list written independently of a treatment produces a set of pictures rather than a piece. You will end up with footage that is individually fine and adds up to nothing, and you will not discover that until you are editing it in week 9.

4 · Storyboard where it earns its place

A storyboard draws the shots so you can see how they sit next to each other.

Outcome 8.2 is storyboard a sequence — not the whole production. That word is doing real work. Storyboards are slow to make, and they are worth it only where the relationships between shots are hard to hold in your head: an action that has to match across a cut, a reveal that depends on what came immediately before, a bit of geography a viewer could get lost in.

Storyboarding everything is a way of avoiding deciding what is difficult. Pick the sequence that is genuinely hard and draw that one properly.

You do not have to be able to draw. Boxes and stick figures carry the information. Nobody is assessing the drawing.

5 · Four roles, four accountabilities

These are not job titles and they are not a way of taking turns with the equipment. Each one owns something, in the sense that if it goes wrong, it is that person's.

Role Owns
Director What the shot is — what is in frame, what the shot is for
Camera The image — exposure, focus, framing, whether it is usable
Sound The recording — levels, placement, whether it is clean
Producer The day — the schedule, the equipment, whether the thing finishes

Producer is the one people misread. Because the visible work is camera and sound, students holding the producer role often stand still, on the reasonable-looking assumption that if nobody needs them, they are not needed.

Producing is deciding what happens next.

You are the person the others are waiting on. When the shot is done, they look at you. When the location is occupied and three people are standing in a corridor, that is yours. When you are half an hour behind, the decision about what to drop is yours.

With three people and four roles, something has to double up. Your team decides which — and writes it down. Left unstated, the ambiguity always resolves itself the same way: nobody did sound. And your sound is not decoration this week. Week 9's outcome 9.3 is syncing separately recorded audio, and if you record into the camera instead, you have deleted one of next week's outcomes for your whole team.

6 · Scheduling is a skill, and nobody tells you that

Sequencing a day is real work with real technique in it:

  • What has to happen before what. You cannot shoot the reaction before you have the thing being reacted to.
  • What can run in parallel. While the director and camera set up the next shot, sound can be getting room tone. Two people idle is a scheduling failure, not a personality one.
  • What happens when something fails. The location is locked. The subject is late. Four people are standing in a corridor and it is now your problem.
  • What to drop. Not everything on the list will get shot. Deciding in advance which shots you would sacrifice is the difference between a shortened shoot and a broken one.

Everyone in your team holds the producer role once, and the rotation is written down before you start. That requirement exists so that outcome 8.3 is assessable for every one of you, rather than for one person per team.

7 · What you are actually being graded on

Say it to yourself now, because in the room it will feel like something else.

This week will feel stressful. Shoots do. The temptation is to read that stress as the shoot went badly rather than as this is the thing I am being assessed on.

You are being graded on your planning documents, and your team's account of who ran what — including the day you were producer.

Not on how good the footage looks. Not on how smoothly it went. On whether the plan was thinking, and on whether you were accountable.

A shoot where the location fell through, you re-sequenced the day, dropped two shots deliberately, and told everyone why, is a better answer than a shoot where nothing went wrong.

Module 4 vocabulary

Term Means
Treatment Short prose stating what the piece is, who it is for, and what it is trying to do. Commits to something, and therefore rules things out
Shot list Every shot you intend to get, in a shootable order, derived from the treatment
Storyboard Drawn shots, for the sequence whose relationships are hard to hold in your head
Planning documents The treatment, shot list, and storyboard together — made before the shoot
Team production The week 8 shoot: three people, rotating roles, one shared result. The only shared-accountability work in this course
Producer The role that owns the day — schedule, equipment, whether it finishes

All are in the course glossary.

One note on a word you will hear me use. The glossary bars paperwork as a name for planning documents, and the course outline's topic line for this week is planning as thinking, not paperwork. Both are right: paperwork is the name of the failure, not a name for the documents. When planning documents get made to satisfy a requirement and never opened again, they have become paperwork. That is the thing this week is against.

Crewing is the one most easily confused with this week. Crewing is working on someone else's shoot in a support role — it comes back in the final project. Team production is shared accountability for one result, and that is only here.

Practise

Run whose call is it? before the session. Sort decisions by whose role owns them, tell a derived shot list line from an invented one, and sequence a day that has gone wrong. Unlimited attempts, no marks.

Round 1 is the one to do properly. A student who cannot say whose call something is cannot hold the producer role, and the rotation is what this entire week rests on.

Check yourself before the session

  1. What makes a treatment different from a description of your idea?
  2. How would you check whether a shot list was actually derived from a treatment?
  3. Why a sequence rather than the whole production, in outcome 8.2?
  4. What does the producer own, and what does that look like when the visible work is camera and sound?
  5. What are you actually being assessed on this week?
  6. Why does your audio have to be recorded separately, and whose week does that protect?

If you cannot answer 5, re-read section 7. Students lose marks on this week by doing the work and never realising which part of it was the subject.

What happens in the session

You plan as a team in the room before anything is shot, then shoot, rotating so that each of you holds the producer role once. Your instructor watches and does not run it for you.
