Week 2 · Composition — Lesson

COMM 260 · Introduction to Digital Media Production

Read this before the session. About 25 minutes.

Last week you learned that a named choice beats a preference. This week you get the names — eleven of them — and you go looking for them in other people's work.

You are not photographing anything this week. That is deliberate, and section 7 explains why.

1 · Composition is a set of decisions, not a set of rules

You have probably met composition as rules. Thirds. Leading lines. Don't centre things.

Forget that framing. Every term below is something a photographer did — a decision with a consequence. Rules tell you what to do. Decisions can be discussed, defended, and be wrong for good reasons.

The question is never did they follow the rule. It is what did this decision do to the picture.

Term The decision

3 · Subject emphasis is not subject placement

This is the week's central distinction and the one that collapses most often.

They sound alike. They are not.

Subject placement Subject emphasis
Where the subject sits What makes it read first
One decision The result of up to five decisions
A centred subject is centrally placed A centred subject may still read second

Emphasis has five means:

Placement · Scale · Focus · Contrast · Light

Placement is one of them.

Why this matters more than it sounds

Learn them as synonyms and you will produce centred subjects and call it emphasis. Then you will wonder why the picture does not work.

A centred subject competing with something brighter or sharper will lose. Nothing about its placement changed — something else in the frame is simply doing more.

You will see this demonstrated with two photographs: one where a dead-centre subject does not read first, and one where a subject at the very edge dominates completely.

Outcome 2.5 asks you to rank the means — which one is doing the most work. "They all work together" is true, and it is not an answer.

4 · Contrast

This is the term week 1 used without defining. Now it has a definition.

Contrast is a difference the eye reads first — bright against dim, sharp against soft, saturated against muted, large against small.

It is not a fourth thing alongside focus, light, and colour. It is how they work. Nothing in an image is bright on its own; it is bright compared to something dimmer.

Contrast is one of the five means of subject emphasis, and it is the one most often doing the work when students think focus is.

5 · Aperture and shutter — only where they change the picture

Two settings, two effects. That is the whole scope this week.

Setting Controls The two ends
Aperture Depth of field Shallow — subject isolated, context lost · Deep — everything legible, nothing emphasised
Shutter How motion appears Frozen — the moment stopped · Blurred — movement conveyed, detail lost

You are not setting these this week. Outcomes 2.3 and 2.4 are recognise and say what it does. You will set them in week 3.

Notice that each has a cost. Shallow depth of field buys isolation and loses the room. Frozen motion buys clarity and loses the sense that anything moved. That is week 1's section 6, arriving in a new place — and it will keep arriving.

6 · Surveyed, not assessed

You will hear these named in the session:

Manual mode · white balance · ISO and noise · RAW versus JPEG · card handling and file management

You are not expected to perform any of them this week, and none of them is on the assignment.

They are named so the words exist and later weeks need not stop to define them. Each returns where it becomes unavoidable — white balance and manual exposure in week 7, where video gives you no auto mode to hide behind.

If you find yourself revising these, stop. They are vocabulary, not this week's work.

7 · Why you are not shooting this week

You cannot compose deliberately until you can see composition in work that already exists.

Students who skip this step spend weeks 3 and 4 making choices by instinct and then cannot explain them — and explaining them is what the final project grades.

This week you are building a reference collection: published images, one per term, each with a defence of why it qualifies.

The defence is the work. An image that obviously demonstrates a term, found in four seconds, teaches you nothing. An image you had to argue for is where the learning is.

Check yourself before the session

  1. What is the difference between subject placement and subject emphasis?
  2. Name the five means of subject emphasis.
  3. A subject sits dead centre and still does not read first. How is that possible?
  4. What does shallow depth of field buy, and what does it cost?
  5. What does motion blur convey, and what does it cost?
  6. Why are you not photographing anything this week?

If you cannot answer 1 or 3, re-read section 3. It is the distinction this week is built on.

What happens in the session

You hunt down published images — other people's work, not your own — that demonstrate each of the eleven terms, and you defend the hard ones out loud. Full detail in the lab sheet.

Then you submit the collection. Full detail in the assignment.
