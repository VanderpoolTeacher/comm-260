Building a Media Producer's Presence — Student Brief

COMM 260 · Final Project · Weeks 12–15 · Individual

What you're making

A social media presence for yourself as a digital media producer — the identity that holds it together, and the photography, audio, and video that fills it. It lives on a landing page you build and publish. You finish by presenting it and defending your decisions.

Why this exists

Everything you've practised this term has been separate: a photo exercise here, an audio exercise there. This project is where you point all of it at one purpose you chose. When you're done you'll have something you can show an employer or a client, and — more importantly — you'll be able to explain why every piece of it looks and sounds the way it does. That second part is what separates a producer from someone who owns a camera.

Exactly what's required

Piece How much Format
Photography set 12 images JPEG, longest edge 2400 px, sRGB
Audio piece 2–3 minutes WAV 48 kHz/24-bit + MP3 320 kbps
Video piece 60–90 seconds H.264 MP4, 1080p, 24 fps
Landing page 1 page HTML, live URL
Positioning statement 300–500 words PDF
Presentation 5–7 minutes Live, plus questions

Under the minimum is incomplete. Over the maximum is not extra credit — editing to length is part of the work.

Two things you don't upload

Worth 15% together, and neither produces a file. They happen in the room. If you skip them there is nothing to submit later, so put them in your calendar now.

Crew log — 10%. Three sessions, three different producers, one per phase across weeks 12–14. You work on classmates' shoots — holding a boom, running a light, second camera. For each: date, whose shoot, your role, what you were responsible for, one thing that went wrong and how it was handled. The producer signs it. Crewing for the same friend three times doesn't count.

Critique — 5%. Two classmates at every checkpoint, spoken, roughly three minutes each. Technical quality, aesthetic design, narrative effect — all three, every time. One specific, actionable thing per dimension.

Phase 1 — Decide how you want to present yourself

Goal: Define the producer you're presenting as, and set the rules your media will follow.

Do this:

  1. Write one sentence: "I make ___ for ___." Fill both blanks. If you can't, you don't have a brand yet — try five versions and pick the one you'd defend.
  2. Choose the professional context you're aiming at. Name it specifically — "wedding videography in northwest Ohio," not "video."
  3. Build the logo.
  4. Write the brand guide: positioning, audience, voice, what you make.
  5. Write the style guide: colour palette, type, framing rules, audio tone, pacing. Numbers and names, not adjectives.
  6. Set up your project folder to the structure below. Do it once, now.
  7. Build your landing page shell and apply your identity to it. You'll fill it each week.

Hand in: Logo, brand guide, style guide, landing page shell, project folder.

You're done when: Your style guide says things like "#C84A1F on #1B2A36. Headlines in Oswald. Shoot f/2.8 or wider, natural light only. Music under 90 BPM. Cut on action, never mid-sentence."

Not things like "clean and modern, warm tones, good pacing." You'll shoot against these rules for three weeks and be graded against them. Vague rules can't be followed or checked.

Watch out for: Spending the whole phase on the logo. It's the smallest part of this and worth the least. The style guide is what the next three weeks depend on.

Phase 2 — Capture

Goal: Produce the photography and audio your presence needs, on brand.

Do this:

  1. List the shots and recordings your presence actually needs. You need 12 usable images and 2–3 minutes of audio, so plan for more.
  2. Write down your creative intent for the photo set before you shoot. One paragraph.
  3. Shoot. Record your capture settings as you go — they go in your tools statement.
  4. Record your audio piece. Keep every raw file; you'll hand those in too.
  5. Edit both to your style guide.
  6. Publish them to your landing page.
  7. Crew for a classmate this phase, and log it.

Hand in: Photo set with tools statement, capture settings, and intent statement. Audio piece with tools statement and raw sources.

You're done when: Your images hit the technical specs and visibly serve the intent you wrote down — both, not either. Your edited audio is measurably cleaner than the raw, and the cuts serve the story rather than just tidying it.

Watch out for: Shooting first and writing the intent afterwards to match what you got. That's backwards, and it's obvious in the submission.

Phase 3 — Video

Goal: Plan, shoot, and edit a video piece for the presence.

Do this:

  1. Plan it before you shoot — shot list, treatment, storyboard, whatever suits the piece. You'll hand this in.
  2. Shoot to the plan.
  3. When you depart from the plan — you will — write down what changed and why.
  4. Edit to your style guide, 60–90 seconds.
  5. Publish it to your landing page.
  6. Crew for a different classmate, and log it.

Hand in: Finished sequence, tools statement, planning documents, edit project file.

You're done when: Someone can hold your plan next to your finished piece and see the relationship — including where you deliberately broke from it.

Watch out for: Shooting without a plan and writing one afterwards. A strong edit of unplanned footage doesn't satisfy "plan, shoot, and edit" — the planning documents are assessed as evidence, not as paperwork.

Phase 4 — Publish and defend

Goal: Bring it together, publish it, and account for it.

Do this:

  1. Finish the landing page — all 12 photographs, the audio piece, the video piece, working.
  2. Decide linear or non-linear, and write why: what your choice makes possible, and what it costs you.
  3. Publish to GitHub Pages and check the live URL works.
  4. Write your positioning statement — the context you're targeting and the conventions you follow.
  5. Complete your disclosure log and get your crew log signed off.
  6. Prepare and give your presentation.

Hand in: Live landing page URL, mode justification, positioning statement, disclosure log, signed crew log, presentation.

You're done when: Removing any one medium from your landing page would break it. If the audio could be swapped out and nobody would notice, you've assembled rather than integrated.

Watch out for: This phase is the heaviest and nothing in it can be done early — you can't integrate media that doesn't exist yet. If you're behind after Phase 3, this is where it hurts.

Linear or non-linear

You have to choose one and defend it.

Linear — one order, start to finish. A visitor scrolls your page top to bottom and meets things in the order you chose. Gains: you control the build. Costs: someone who wants the video first has to hunt.

Non-linear — a hub. A visitor enters anywhere and picks their own route. Gains: a hiring manager reaches the reel in one click. Costs: you can't build anything.

Both work for a landing page, which is why you have to argue for yours. Name what your choice makes possible and what it costs. Naming only the upside doesn't count.

Your project folder

Set this up in Phase 1 and keep everything in it.

comm260-presence-lastname/
├── 00_brand/          logo files, brand guide, style guide
├── 01_photo/
│   ├── raw/           every frame you shot — never delete
│   ├── edited/        final exports
│   └── capture-settings.md
├── 02_audio/
│   ├── raw/           untouched recordings — never delete
│   ├── project/       your edit session
│   └── edited/
├── 03_video/
│   ├── raw/
│   ├── planning/      shot list, treatment, storyboard
│   ├── project/
│   └── edited/
├── 04_docs/           positioning statement, crew log, disclosure log
└── site/              index.html, css, images — your landing page

Name files comm260-lastname-piece.ext — comm260-vega-audio.wav. Lowercase, hyphens, no spaces. Spaces break on web servers even when they work on your laptop.

Publishing the page

  1. Create a free GitHub account and a public repository named lastname-presence.
  2. Put your site/ contents at the top of the repository — index.html must be there.
  3. Settings → Pages → deploy from the main branch.
  4. Submit the live URL: lastname.github.io/lastname-presence

Your page stays live after the course ends, so the link keeps working when you send it to someone. We walk through this in class during Phase 1 — you don't need to know Git beforehand.

Open your own URL before you submit it. If images are missing there but fine on your laptop, your file paths or capitalisation are wrong — servers care about capital letters, your laptop does not.

The presentation

5–7 minutes, plus questions. Your live landing page on the screen — not slides about it. Walk us through the page as a visitor would move through it. Play the audio and the video.

Cover: who you are as a producer, the identity you built, the three pieces, and how the page structures them. For each one, say why it is the way it is — and what you considered and rejected.

Expect to be asked "why not the other way?" about any decision — including ones you didn't plan to discuss. Knowing what you chose is half the job; knowing what you turned down is the other half.

Scored on its own rubric — see the Presentation Rubric. Your presentation grade is independent of your project grade. A strong presentation won't rescue thin media, and strong media won't carry a presentation you can't defend.

How you'll be graded

Component Weight
Brand foundation (logo, brand guide, style guide) 10%
Photography set 10%
Audio piece 10%
Video piece 15%
Landing page 20%
Positioning statement 5%
Crew log 10%
Critique participation 5%
Presentation 15%

Two rubrics: the Project Rubric covers everything except the presentation; the Presentation Rubric covers that.

This project is 40% of your course grade. The percentages above are shares of the project. So the landing page, at 20% of the project, is 8% of your course grade.

Note the brand foundation is 10%. It's the container everything else hangs on, but it isn't the work — a beautiful logo with thin media won't pass.

Tools statement — on every piece you hand in

With your photography, your audio, and your video you submit a tools statement: what equipment and software you used, what settings you chose, and why you chose them for that situation.

Counts "f/2.8 to drop the cluttered background behind her"
Doesn't count "f/2.8, 1/250, ISO 400"

Listing settings is record-keeping. Explaining them is the thing being assessed. A piece with a settings list and no reasons cannot reach Meets on its rubric row, however good the media is.

Keep notes as you shoot. Reconstructing this a week later is how students end up guessing at their own decisions.

Disclosure

Every submission includes a disclosure log: source material you used, and any AI assistance — what it contributed, and what you did to check the result.

This isn't a formality. Part of your grade is whether the work is original, and originality can only be judged against what it came from. A component with no disclosure can't be assessed on that criterion at all.
