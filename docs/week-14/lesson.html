1 · Weeks 7 to 9, alone

This phase Was
Treatment, shot list derived from it, storyboard Week 8 — in a team
Camera configured with reasons, coverage, separately recorded audio Weeks 7 and 8
Sync, cut on action, export to specification, project file Week 9

Nothing technical here is new. What is different is that you are alone.

In week 8 someone else was the producer for two-thirds of the shoot, and when you held the role, two people were waiting on you. That pressure is also what kept the day moving. On your own there is nobody to be behind for, and the day quietly expands until the light goes.

Schedule your own shoot day as if somebody were waiting on you. That is the week 8 skill, applied to yourself, and nobody will tell you when you are failing at it.

2 · The one new thing: departures

Outcome 14.2 is identify departures from your plan as decisions, with reasons. You have not met this before, and it is what this phase is really about.

There are three ways to relate to a plan and only one of them is right.

Produces
Follow it rigidly A piece that ignores what the location actually turned out to be
Abandon it silently A piece with no traceable reasoning behind any of it
Depart deliberately, and say so What outcome 14.2 asks for

Why rigid is not the safe option

Students assume the graded behaviour is compliance. It is not.

Your treatment said the workshop would be busy. You arrive and it is empty and quiet, and it turns out to be a better piece about solitude than it ever would have been about bustle. Shooting your shot list anyway, because it is your shot list, is the worst of the three outcomes — you have ignored the only real information the day gave you.

Why silent is worse than it looks

If you depart and do not say so, your planning documents and your finished piece do not obviously relate. That is indistinguishable from never having planned at all, and the assessment is whether the finished piece is traceable to the plan.

What a departure looks like written down

"I planned five interior shots and got three. The room was smaller than I remembered and two of the angles did not exist. I used the extra time on the exterior instead, which the treatment had one line about and the finished piece now opens with."

That is a decision. It says what the location turned out to be, what was dropped, and what was chosen instead.

Compare:

"Some shots did not work out."

That is not a departure. It is an apology, and it evidences nothing.

The tell

A plan written after the shoot has no departures in it, because it was written to match what was captured. This is the same failure as intent-after-shooting in weeks 3 and 13, and here it is the easiest of the three to detect.

3 · Departures are not failures — say them plainly

Students apologise for not following the plan.

Outcome 14.2 treats a departure as a decision. The reason is what is assessed, not the compliance.

There is no penalty in this course for a plan that changed. There is a penalty for a plan that changed and was not noticed.

4 · Your style guide constrains rhythm, not just look

The pacing rules you wrote in week 12 — maximum shot length, cut speed, whether music sits under speech, how long a still is held — are the part of your guide you have never had to obey.

Go and read your own document before you cut. Students obey their visual identity faithfully and forget the guide governs rhythm too, then discover at the checkpoint that their video complies with none of the rules they wrote for it.

And the same conflict rule from week 13 applies. If a pacing rule turns out to be unworkable, revise it and record it. Silently ignoring it is the option that is not available.

5 · The project file is submitted, and it has to open

Component D is submitted with its project file. Not just the exported video.

Week 9's outcome 9.2 is what makes that file openable — media in one place, bins named for what is in them, project stored with its media.

A project file that will not open evidences nothing.

Not "loses marks." Nothing. Outcome 14.1 is plan, shoot, and edit, and the edit is only inspectable from the project.

Your project file gets opened at the checkpoint, in front of you. A broken project caught there is fixable. Caught in week 15 it is not, and it costs you the heaviest component in the project.

6 · Crewing — the last chance

Outcome 14.3. Third session, third producer.

This is the final phase in which the crew log can be completed. Three sessions across weeks 12–14, three different producers, each signed on the day.

If you have duplicated a producer across phases 1 and 2, this is the week to fix it — there is no phase four for crewing.

7 · Thanksgiving costs this phase three sessions

The heaviest interruption in the term, and it lands on the heaviest single component.

You are producing rather than being taught, so what is lost is contact time, not content. The material was delivered in weeks 7 to 9 and none of it has gone anywhere. What disappears is having somebody to ask while you are stuck.

Two things this changes for you:

  1. Book equipment for the whole phase, including the days nobody is on campus. The desk closes. The booking calendar does not stretch afterwards.
  2. Do not plan to shoot during the break unless your subject has already confirmed they will be there. People are away, buildings are locked, and a shoot day nobody attends is not recoverable inside this phase.

Your checkpoint feedback will arrive compressed against week 15. Whatever you can finish before the break, finish before the break.

Module 5 vocabulary

No new terms. These are the ones that matter here, all in the course glossary:

Term Means

Planning documents Shot list, treatment, or storyboard — made before the shoot
Project file The editing application's session file
Crewing · Crew log Working on a classmate's shoot; the record of it, signed by the producer

Two bars. Video piece is barred from film, reel, video story, and movie. Planning documents is barred from pre-production, prep, plan alone — and from paperwork, except as the name of the failure, which is week 8's fixed topic line.

Practise

Run departure or drift? before the phase begins. Sort real accounts into the three behaviours, tell a departure from an apology, and spot a plan written after the shoot.

Round 2 is the one to do properly. It is departure-versus-apology, which is exactly what checkpoint 3 reads your account against.

Check yourself before the phase

  1. Name the three ways to relate to a plan, and which one is assessed.
  2. Why is following the plan rigidly not the safe option?
  3. What is the difference between a departure and an apology?
  4. How can a marker tell a plan was written after the shoot?
  5. Which part of your style guide have you not yet had to obey?
  6. What happens if your project file does not open?

If you cannot answer 3, re-read section 2. It is what the checkpoint reads your account against.

What happens in the phase

The three behaviours get named at the start, and the break gets planned in the room — shoot days, equipment return, when the checkpoint falls. Then you produce, alone, to your own style guide.

At the checkpoint your project file is opened in front of you and you are asked, explicitly, what you planned that you did not do.
