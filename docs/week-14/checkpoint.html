Checkpoint 3 — Video piece

COMM 260 · Introduction to Digital Media Production

Formative checkpoint · project rubric, component D

15% of the project · 6% of the course

The heaviest of the three media components — it carries plan, shoot, and edit in one.

What to submit

  1. The sequence — 60–90 seconds, exported to the stated specification
  2. Planning documents — treatment, shot list derived from it, storyboard
  3. The project file, with its media
  4. Your account of departures — what you planned that you did not do, and why
  5. Third crew log entry, signed, with a different producer from phases 1 and 2
  6. Disclosure log, updated

Plus, in the room: critique across all three dimensions. Component H.

How this is graded

Assessed on whether the finished piece is traceable to the plan, departures included and explained.

Traceable does not mean identical. A piece that matches its plan exactly is not the target — it usually means the plan was written afterwards. What is being read is whether the finished thing can be followed back to the document, with the differences accounted for.

What a complete answer looks like

The departures account is the part students get wrong. Here is one that would be marked Meets:

What I planned. A 75-second piece about the last hour of a shift at the bike workshop: five interior shots of him closing up, two exteriors, and a slow wide of the empty room at the end. The treatment said the piece was about the room emptying out.

Departure 1 — three interiors instead of five. The workshop is narrower than I remembered from scouting and two of my planned angles were against a blank wall with nothing in the frame. I dropped both rather than shoot them for the sake of the list. What I did instead: used the time on a close of him locking the roller door, which was not on the shot list at all and is now the second-to-last shot.

Departure 2 — the ending changed. I planned to end on the slow wide of the empty room. I shot it, and it does not work — the room is not visually empty enough for the shot to read as emptiness. The piece now ends on the exterior with the light off. What it cost: the treatment's argument was about the room, and ending outside makes it slightly more about him. I accepted that; the alternative was an ending that did not land.

Departure 3 — from my own process. I storyboarded the whole thing and only used the boards for the closing sequence. The rest did not need them, and drawing them was time I should have spent scouting — which is what caused departure 1.

Style guide. No shot over 6 seconds, per my pacing rules, except the final exterior which holds for 9. That is a deliberate breach and I have recorded it: the piece needed one shot that outstays its welcome slightly, and it is the last one.

What that demonstrates: every departure names what the location or subject turned out to be, what was dropped, and what was chosen instead. Departure 3 is about process rather than content and it is the most useful one in the account. And the pacing breach is declared rather than hoped past.

To reach Exceeds, name what a departure gave up more sharply. Departure 1 is close: "dropping both interiors means the piece has less of the room in it than the treatment promised, and a viewer who has never been there gets a smaller space than the real one."

How the checkpoint runs

1 Critique in the room, all three dimensions — component H
2 The piece read against the plan — planning documents and sequence side by side
3 You are asked for your departures, explicitly
4 The piece checked against your style guide's pacing rules
5 Your project file is opened. Not received — opened
6 Third crew log entry verified, and all three producers confirmed different

What does not count

  • No departures reported. Almost never true. Every shoot departs from its plan — locations differ from memory, people are late, light moves.
  • Departures reported as failures. "Some shots didn't work out" is an apology. Outcome 14.2 treats a departure as a decision, and the reason is what is assessed.
  • A plan written after the shoot. Detectable, because it contains no departures — it was written to match what was captured.
  • Pacing rules ignored. Your style guide governs rhythm too. An undeclared breach is not a decision.
  • A project file that does not open. Media linked from locations that no longer exist. Discovered here it is recoverable; discovered in week 15 it is not.
  • A third crew log entry with a producer you have already crewed for. Three phases, three different producers, and this is the last one.

Use the glossary

This week: Video piece, Planning documents, Project file, Crewing, Crew log, Checkpoint, Deliberate choice, Cost.

Two bars. Video piece is barred from film, reel, video story, and movie. Project file is barred from timeline, session, edit file, and working file — which matters here, because the thing being submitted is the session, not the timeline inside it.

Why the project file gets opened in front of you

It is the single most valuable five minutes of this checkpoint.

A broken project caught here is fixable — you have the media, you have the phase, and you can rebuild the links. Caught in week 15 it is not fixable, and outcome 14.1 is plan, shoot and edit: the edit is only inspectable from the project.

This is week 9's outcome 9.2 arriving exactly where week 9 said the bill would come due.

Rubric

LO 4 — Video

Plan, shoot, and edit high-definition video using professional production techniques.

Level
Exceeds Departures are clearly decisions — you can say what the location or subject turned out to be, and what you chose instead
Meets Plans, shoots, and edits. The piece is traceable to the plan and departures are named with reasons
Approaching Plan and piece do not obviously relate, or no departures are reported
Not yet No plan, or a plan written after the shoot — detectable because it contains no departures

LO 1 — Aesthetic and narrative principles

Apply aesthetic and narrative principles to create compelling digital media projects.

Level
Exceeds Names the choice, what it accomplishes, and what the alternative would have cost
Meets Names the choice in course vocabulary and says what it accomplishes
Approaching Describes the work accurately but does not name the choice — or names the technique without saying what it does
Not yet States a preference. "It looked better."

LO 9 — Collaboration

Collaborate effectively in production teams, demonstrating communication and project management skills.

Level
Exceeds The producer day shows sequencing decisions — what ran in parallel, what changed when something failed
Meets Held a defined role and the producer role. The team can say what you were responsible for
Approaching Present and contributing, but accountability is unclear or was never held
Not yet Teammates cannot say what you were responsible for

On LO 9 here: crewing evidences the collaboration half only, as in week 13. The project management half is evidenced in week 8 and nowhere else.

The same four levels apply to every assignment in this course. Full descriptors and the whole map of which assignment evidences what: competency rubric.
